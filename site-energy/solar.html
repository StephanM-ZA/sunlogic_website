Do I need a battery?

Usually, yes, and it's worth knowing why rather than just being sold one.

Your house uses most of its electricity early in the morning and again in the evening. Your panels produce in the middle of the day, when the house is often empty. Without somewhere to put that daytime power, you sell it back to the grid for a fraction of what you pay to buy it back at supper time.

A unit of electricity you use yourself is worth considerably more than one you sell back, because it's bought from you for a great deal less than it's sold to you. A battery is simply what lets you keep it.

If there's an electric car in the picture, that changes the sums in your favour. A car is the one large load whose timing is entirely up to you: it doesn't care whether it charges at two in the afternoon or two in the morning, only that it's full when you leave. Charge it while the sun is up and you're running it on power you made rather than power you bought.

 The bit nobody mentions: selling power back can put you on a tariff that costs you more. To sell surplus power back you generally have to move onto a different tariff, and that tariff carries a much higher fixed monthly charge in exchange for a cheaper rate per unit. The better your solar works, the less that trade is worth. For some households the right answer is a slightly smaller system and no tariff change at all. We check before we quote.

A home has to buy a battery to store daytime power for the evening. A business is usually open and using it as it's produced. Batteries are the single most expensive part of a system, so needing less of one often means a business gets a better return on a cheaper system.

What it costs, and what it changes

It depends almost entirely on whether you need storage. A rooftop system without a battery costs considerably less than the same system with one, and for most daytime businesses that's exactly the right trade.

We quote from what your business actually consumed, not from your floor area and not off a satellite photo.

If you run vehicles, say so at the assessment. A van or a bakkie that charges while you trade is charging in the exact hours your panels produce most, and that is the cheapest fuel you will ever put in it. Planning for it now costs far less than adding capacity later.

One thing to check early

Most municipalities put a ceiling on how much solar you're permitted to feed back on a standard single-phase supply. Going beyond it means upgrading the supply, which is a council charge and a council waiting period, not something an installer controls.

It's far better to find that out during the first site visit than after you have paid a deposit, so it's one of the first things we look at.
