01

A system, not a style guide

Sunlogic installs solar and does the paperwork. The identity has one job: look like a contractor who knows the trade and will tell you the truth about your roof. Warm, plain, unhurried. Nothing here is decorative for its own sake.

Warm, not clinical

Surfaces are beige, not white and not grey. The whole palette sits on the warm side, including the greys.

Flat, not layered

Depth comes from a tonal step and a hairline, never a shadow. The page reads as printed matter, not as floating cards.

One accent, rationed

A single orange. Used once or twice per screen, it means something. Used everywhere, it means nothing.

Honest about gaps

Unknown facts show as visible bracketed placeholders. A missing photograph is a labelled empty state, never stock.

Case

Hero, section and CTA headings are Title Case at any length. A card or step heading is Title Case up to five words and sentence case past that. Prose is always sentence case. The copy document remains the authority on wording.

Do: structure

Title Case for hero and section headings and the closing CTA, however long they run. These are the page's own structure. "Solar That Pays For Itself." Minor words stay lower case (a, and, at, for, in, of, on, or, the, to) unless they open the heading.

Do: cards, five words or fewer

Title Case, because a short heading is a label and reads as the name of a thing. "Straight Numbers." "Backup Power." "One Team, Both Jobs."

Do: cards, past five words

Sentence case, because it has stopped being a label and become a sentence. "We will take on a system we did not install." A grid of four Title Cased sentences shouts four times. This applies to article titles too, and a title is set the same way on its card and on its own page.

Do: prose

Sentence case for sub-heads inside body copy, eyebrows, field labels and captions. "What will it actually save me?"

Do

Set the final phrase of a headline in orange, once per heading.

Don't

Colour a phrase mid-sentence, or use the device twice in one heading. And never ALL CAPS a heading. Capitals belong to the mono face at label size.

One rhythm, two kinds of band

A full-bleed section gives 96px from its own edge to its content. An inset panel band, meaning the statement, the closing CTA and the division promo, reaches the same figure by splitting it: 40px of band padding plus the panel's own padding. The band supplies the gap that shows the panel is inset; the panel supplies the rest.

What goes wrong

Giving the band a full section padding and letting the panel keep its own. The two stack, the statement gets close to double the rhythm above its first line, and the page reads as though it has been pulled apart. This shipped once and it is the reason the panel gap token exists.

Do

Use var(--panel-gap) on the wrapper, never var(--section-pad-y). A wrapper is not a section.

The one exception: a sector marker

A dl-sector marker belongs to the section it opens, so a full rhythm between the two reads as a gap inside a heading. The marker and its section sit at three quarters of the rhythm: 72px desktop, 42px below 768px. A full rhythm and a half were both tried on the page; one looked disconnected and the other cramped. It is written as a ratio rather than a pixel value so it holds at every breakpoint on its own, and it is the only place the rhythm bends.

A split with one child is not a split

Remove a column from cols="split" and the wrapper is usually left behind, which leaves half the band empty at every width above 768px. Remove the wrapper too. The stylesheet collapses a one-child split to a single column so it never looks broken, but that is a net, not the fix.

Section rhythm is automatic

Every top-level block inside a section sits 40px from the next: heading stack, grid, callout, form. It is a property of the section, not something anyone applies. A heading can never butt up against the grid below it, and nobody has to remember a margin.

Cards in a row are always level

Every card in a grid stretches to the height of its tallest sibling, whatever the copy length. Ragged card bottoms are the most common way a grid stops looking designed, so it is enforced by the card rather than left to the page.

Matching card heights isn't enough on its own: a card's dl-actions row sits flush with the bottom edge too (.sl-card > dl-actions { margin-top: auto }), so buttons land level across the row instead of trailing wherever the copy above them happens to end. Don't add a fixed height, a spacer, or per-card margin to force this by hand; the rule already does it for any card with a button row.

05

Four behaviours, nothing else

Gradient drift, light sweep, fade-up, pulse. Motion in this system is slow and atmospheric, never reactive. Nothing bounces, nothing springs, nothing scales on hover.

Getting back to the top

Every page carries a back-to-top button, rendered by dl-dock so no page can be added without one. It appears once a quarter of the page has scrolled past — a percentage, not a pixel count, because these pages run from 800px to 8,400px and “after 600px” would put it on screen immediately on the short ones, where there is nothing to go back up to. A page shorter than its own viewport never shows it.

It sits above the dock on mobile and takes the bottom-right corner on desktop: the dock is centred and grows to nearly the full width on a narrow screen, so the corner is not free until there is room for both. The scroll is smooth unless the reader has asked for reduced motion, and focus returns to the skip link so the next Tab resumes at the top of the page rather than the bottom of one they have just left.

The hero needs all five layers

Photograph, 14s gradient drift, 9s light sweep, side scrim, bottom scrim. The drift alone reads static in a single glance. The sweep is what makes motion legible over a photograph. The side scrim is what holds text contrast against a bright image nobody has seen yet.

Live, over an empty slot

Do

Honour prefers-reduced-motion: animations resolve to their end state rather than disappearing.

Don't

Add particles, parallax, spring physics or hover scaling. None are in the vocabulary.

Paired buttons can fill different colours

Where two buttons sit side by side and each should read as its own thing, wipe sets which colour fills. On the home page Solar fills orange and Electrical fills navy, so the pair is distinguishable before you read the labels. Only these two values exist. It is not a licence to introduce a colour.

Solar Electrical

50px tall, 38px small. 4px radius. Press is a 1px downward translate. Every button wipes on hover: a fill rising bottom-to-top over 240ms. It sits on the base button rather than a variant, so it is automatic wherever a button appears. Navy, beige and outline wipe to orange; the orange emphasis button wipes to navy; ghost-on-dark wipes to a restrained white. One emphasis button in view: the closing CTA is the allowed second.

Cards, steps and stats

When a card goes navy. Exactly one place: the final step of a numbered sequence, so the process closes on a full stop rather than trailing off. Never an interior step, never two in one sequence, and never as decoration on an ordinary card. It is navy #0D2028, not a second accent, and it carries meaning only because it is rationed. Elsewhere a dark card belongs to a dl-statement band or the closing CTA.

The stat value slot is 40px at weight 900, sized for numerals and short values. For long word values at phone width, the same facts restate as a label/value row instead.

Cards hover

The hairline alone. The border warms over 100ms and nothing else moves: no fill change, no lift, no shadow, no scale. A card that is itself a link takes the orange edge instead. It is on the card, so it applies everywhere.

Which skill owns which job

Copy is not written by feel here. Every kind of copy has a skill that owns it, and the skill is run, not remembered. Applying a skill's principles from memory is not the same thing and must never be reported as if it were.

Do

Verify, then persuade. A claim about how somebody else charges, taxes or regulates does not go on a page without a document behind it. Knowing a claim is true is permission to say it plainly, not an instruction to cite it.

Don't

Hedge an unsourced claim. Hedging keeps the risk and loses the force. Cut it instead, and put it back when the source exists.

Not the problem

Marketing and sales language. This is a site that has to convert interest into enquiries. The rule is about unverified facts, never about persuasive writing.

Hover is a promise

Hover state and the pointer cursor both say that something happens. On a block you cannot act on they teach a reader that the signal means nothing, and the reader then stops trusting it on the elements where it does.

Do

A card hovers if it is a link or contains one. Written as .sl-card:has(a, button, summary):hover, so it follows the content rather than the author's memory. Enforced from the other side by rule 17, which fails a pointer cursor on anything that is not a control.

Don't

Give a callout, a statement or a plain content card a hover. A callout also takes no hover at all: the generic card hover set border-color on every side and turned its 3px accent edge grey.

Marks are icons, never characters

A disclosure marker, an arrow, a plus: all icons from the set. Never a typed character in a content: property. A glyph centres on its font's metrics rather than on its box, cannot be restyled with the set, and is not in the vocabulary. Two shipped inside disclosure controls before rule 16 existed. Letters and digits are fine: a step number, a counter, a label.

One card tone per section

Tone belongs to the band, not the card. White steps up from a beige ground; warm does not. A section that runs a row of white cards and then a row of warm ones reads as two design systems sharing a page, and that is what a reader notices before anything else. Navy remains the final step of a numbered sequence and nothing else. Enforced by rule 15, which counts content cards only: a callout is the highlight tint by definition and a stat is glass by definition, so neither is a tone choice.

Highlight

The callout is the highlight block, and the tint is not optional. Its whole job is to interrupt, so a callout that sits at the same value as the band behind it is a bug rather than a quieter choice. Tinted surface, a 3px rule, an orange icon, and copy at lead size rather than the 16px default. The tint is roughly 7% orange in beige, never the accent colour itself: a solid orange block competes with the page's one emphasis button. Use it where a page has to stop and say one thing. Not more than one or two per page.

The part most people miss

It is not only the rate per unit going up. The fixed monthly charge has been climbing faster than the rate.

Statement band

One large line with the dawn gradient drifting behind it. This is what breaks up a long service page: a beat between two runs of prose. One per two or three sections; more and it stops being a break. It is an inset panel at the 16px radius, the same shape as the closing CTA and the promo card, not a full-bleed band: every dark block on a page wears the same corner.

Article cards

A card pointing at an article carries a cover image and a publish date. The date sits above the heading in the mono face at label size, because when something was written is part of deciding whether to read it. Pass it as date="2026-08-30" and the component prints it and writes the <time datetime>. The cover is the subject of the piece, never a product shot.

Navigation, and the dock

Two layers of navigation, and one action. The header is sticky, 72px, four links plus the quote button. The section nav appears only on pages that serve several audiences in one scroll, sits under the header at 72px, and marks the section you are in.

Nav links take the accent on hover and when current: orange, except Electrical, which takes navy. That is the same pairing as the Solar/Electrical buttons, so the two trades read consistently wherever they appear together. When one link is current the rest drop to 55% opacity and come back to full on hover.

The dock is not a second nav. Repeating the header at the bottom of the page earns nothing. It carries the things someone actually wants within reach while reading a long page (a way to reach us and the quote), at 4px corners and with the same hover wipe as every other button. The contact slot rolls between both phone numbers and the email address, one visible at a time, sliding up every 3 seconds (<dl-roll>: the same component handles every rolling phone number on the site, not just the dock's).

The savings calculator, the review carousel and the day feed are third-party web components with their own markup. Do

Theme a plugin in the PLUGIN THEME section of shared/sunlogic.css, at :root specificity so it beats the style block the plugin injects at runtime.

Don't

Edit the plugin's JavaScript to restyle it. The next version overwrites you, and the plugin stops tracking the system.

Do

Bump the ?v= on every tag that loads a file you have just changed: the plugins and everything in shared/. There is no build step, so a browser will serve a cached copy of the old file and the page runs stale code without erroring.

Don't

Theme a plugin element that is an <a> tag without opting it out of the system's own link-dim rule. Plain text links dim to 70% opacity on hover (a:hover); every button and card is explicitly excepted (a.sl-btn:hover, a.sl-card:hover, …). A plugin card left off that list silently washes out on hover along with whatever hover state you did theme. A review card once dimmed exactly this way. Add opacity: 1 to the plugin's own hover rule in the PLUGIN THEME section, not to the central exception list.

Someone who knows the trade

Knowledgeable rather than technical. Not a friendly stranger, not a brochure. A contractor explaining what they found on your roof.

Do

"If solar is not worth it for your property yet, we will say so."

Don't

"The Western Cape's leading solar solutions provider."

Do

Explain a Certificate of Compliance in one clause, the first time it appears on a page.

Don't

Quote a payback period. It depends on tariff assumptions nobody can promise.
