Load shedding

Scheduled load shedding gave you one thing the current situation doesn't: notice. A published schedule meant you knew the power was going off at six, and you could do something about it at half past five.

Outages didn't stop. A substation fault, a storm, stolen cable or planned maintenance all end in the same dark kitchen, and none of them arrive with a timetable. Which makes the habits below more useful now than they were then, not less, because you no longer get the warning.

Five things worth doing, cheapest first.

  • Keep the important things charged. Laptop, phone, torch, the router if it has a battery. The point isn't to charge when an outage is announced. It's to not be flat when one isn't.
  • Unplug what a surge would kill. The damage usually happens when the power comes back, not when it goes off. Anything expensive and sensitive is better unplugged than trusting a multiplug. A UPS is the proper version of this: it doesn't add power, it carries the load through the gap without a break, which is what keeps a desktop or a router from dropping at all.
  • Freeze bottles of water. Fill a few plastic bottles and keep them in the freezer. Move them to the fridge when the power goes. They hold the cold for hours, they cost nothing, and they're the reason your fridge survives an afternoon outage without you throwing anything away.
  • Deal with the geyser. It's the biggest single user of electricity in most homes, and it heats on a timer whether anyone's home or not. Insulating it slows the standing losses so the element runs less to hold temperature. Switching it manually works too, and it works right up until the day you forget, which is why we'd rather put a controller on it. That's what our hot water page is about.
  • Then look at a system. Panels with an inverter and a battery will run the house through an outage and cut what you buy the rest of the time. You don't have to disconnect from the grid to get that, and you don't have to buy it all at once.

    On that last point, going fully off-grid costs more than staying connected, because a system with no grid behind it has to carry the worst week of the year on batteries alone. “But you don’t need to go completely off-grid to benefit from a solar system,” Craig Lewis of Sunlogic said when this was first published. “You can start small with a backup generator or batteries, and add to your system as needed.” That's still how we build them. We've written up the difference properly in Off the Grid vs. Grid-tied.
