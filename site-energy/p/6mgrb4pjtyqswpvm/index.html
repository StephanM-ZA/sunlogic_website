What We Are Proposing

Attention: Plentify

Re: Smart Geyser and Solar PV Solution for IHS, Scottsdene Residential Development, Cape Town.

Thank you for the request for quotation. We have worked from the RFQ, the 2019 council plans, the signed deviation council pack of 20 May 2026, the site development plan (SD-P9B-E3830 Rev E), the schedule of finishes and the site photographs of June and August 2026. Where the deviation pack changed the design, we have followed it.

We propose one solar system for the whole development: 404 LONGi 615 W bifacial modules on the existing tiled roofs feeding two three-phase grid-tied inverters, each with a Plentify SolarBot, at zero export and with no batteries. All 136 geysers move to a central, landlord-metered geyser supply built by the site electrical contractor, and a Plentify HotBot on every geyser schedules heating against the site’s solar output. The geysers are the store.

The roof sets what is possible here. The deviation pack changed the roofs from mono-pitch zinc to dual-pitch concrete tile at 20°, 8.69 m wide on blocks 1 to 3 and 6.95 m on block 4, with the stair cores under flat concrete slabs. Our array layout is traced from the roof plans in that pack and fits 404 modules on the tiles. With one pool of solar and 136 geysers to heat in turn, our model loses little midday output to full tanks and puts the development at about 82% annual offset against the 75% target. The final rational calculation will be made on surveyed roof faces.

The drawings already commit the project to 50% of hot water from solar-assisted heating under SANS 10400-XA. This system is designed to meet that, and the RFQ’s higher 75% target.

Moving the geysers off the tenants’ prepaid meters means the savings reach tenants through billing, using HotBot’s per-geyser metering, or through hot water included in the rent. That choice sits with IHS, and we set out what each needs.

Your contact for everything on this proposal is Stephan Marais (stephan@sunlogic.co.za). He handles the PV design, the site, the PV certification and the handover, and you will not be passed between people.

The solution

How the System Works

One Solar Pool

404 LONGi 615 W bifacial modules (about 248 kWp) on the tiled roofs, collected by roof face into a DC combiner per block and fed to two Deye SUN-110K-G03 110 kW three-phase grid-tied inverters.

Smart Control Throughout

A Plentify SolarBot and a Deye meter on each inverter hold the site at zero export. A Plentify HotBot on every geyser heats the 136 tanks in turn to use the solar as it arrives. Plentify supplies both.

Measured, Not Estimated

Both inverters report to our VoltIQ monitoring platform, alongside Plentify’s platform for SolarBots and HotBots, so the offset is shown rather than claimed.

The layout

Two Inverters, One Geyser Supply

Why two inverters

248 kWp of modules on two Deye 110 kW string inverters gives a DC-to-AC ratio of about 1.13. With six MPPT inputs per inverter, every roof face gets its own input, half the solar output continues if one fails, and both are expected to sit within the SolarBot-supported range, subject to Plentify’s confirmation. A single inverter of that size is possible only if Plentify confirms SolarBot supports it.

The central geyser supply

A main geyser board with a landlord bulk meter at the inverter room, sub-mains to a geyser board in each block, all by the site electrical contractor. The electrical contractor also reroutes each geyser circuit from the unit board in the service duct. Done now, while the blocks are open, this is simpler and cheaper than after occupation.

Tenant savings

Either tenants are billed only for the grid energy their own geyser draws, from HotBot’s per-geyser metering where it is accepted for billing, or hot water is included in the rent or levy. IHS chooses; both are compatible with the system as designed.

Grid outages

Grid-tied inverters shut down when the grid is off, as the grid code requires. During load-shedding there is no solar heating; HotBot reheats once the grid returns. The final calculation deducts expected outage hours.

The design basis

What the Roof Allows

Four blocks, two roof widths

Four blocks of four storeys hold 136 units, 34 per floor across the site: 135 two-bed and one one-bed. Every roof is dual-pitch concrete tile at 20° on SA pine trusses. Blocks 1 to 3 are 8.69 m wide; the three wings of block 4 are 6.95 m wide. The stair cores sit under flat concrete slabs with 170 mm parapets, set into the courtyard side of each roof.

404 modules, placed on the drawn roofs

Each module is placed within the roof outlines taken from the linework of the deviation pack, clear of the ridges and stair-core slabs, with 0.35 m clear at the eaves, 0.3 m at the ridge and 0.4 m at gables and slabs. The 8.69 m roofs take three landscape rows per face; block 4’s 6.95 m roofs take two. The flat slabs stay in reserve.

About 82% annual offset

True north sits about 35° off the site plan. Blocks 1 to 3 face north-east and south-west, with north-north-west and south-south-east faces on their short wings; block 4’s diagonal wing faces east and west. In our model, October to March is fully covered and June falls to about 48%. If vents and wind-zone edges cost 10% of the modules, the offset is still about 78%.

Fitted to a finished roof

The August 2026 photographs show the roofs tiled and scaffolding still up on the long blocks. We fix tile hooks to the trusses beneath the tiles, share the scaffolding while it stands, and run cables down the service ducts before their steel doors are fitted. Every unit is separated by a firewall sealed to the underside of the tiles; cables cross them only through fire-rated sleeves.

The geyser supply is the decision

Moving 136 geysers to a landlord bulk supply needs IHS’s agreement, a supply sized with HotBot load control, and the City’s approval of the supply, applied for by the site electrical contractor. The bulk supply, and any upgrade to it, is by the site electrical contractor and the City, outside this price.
