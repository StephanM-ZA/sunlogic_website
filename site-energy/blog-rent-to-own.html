Solar

The objection to solar is almost never that it doesn't work. It's the number at the front. A system is bought once, at today's price, and goes on producing for years afterwards, which is exactly where the return comes from and exactly why it's hard to start.

“There’s a perception that solar power is too expensive for the average homeowner or business owner,” Craig Lewis of Sunlogic said when this was first published. “Many people still believe they need to invest in a full system with multiple components to enjoy the benefits of having access to renewable energy.” Neither is necessarily true, and there is more than one way to pay.

Renting and rent-to-own are not the same thing

They get used interchangeably and they answer different questions.

Renting is what it sounds like. You pay monthly, the system stays ours, and maintenance and repairs can sit inside the rental. “Rented systems are useful for tenants who don’t own their home, consumers who would like an immediate solution and may not be able to buy a full system,” Lewis said at the time. If you might move, or you want the power without the asset, this is the one.

Rent-to-own is a route to owning it. You pay monthly over an agreed term, and the point of the arrangement is that you end up with the system rather than handing it back. It costs more in total than paying cash, the way anything paid off over time does, and in exchange you get a working system now instead of in three years.

Terms and what it costs

Terms run over 36, 48 or 60 months, or longer where it suits the job. What you pay a month depends on the size of the system, and the size of the system depends on what your house actually uses rather than on how big your roof is.

As a rough guide to sizing, a household staying under about 900kWh a month is usually looking at the medium grid-tied system, and one above that at the large. For a figure rather than a bracket, our solar calculator works off your monthly bill in a couple of minutes, and a site visit turns that into an actual number.

The other two ways to pay

If you want to own it outright and can't pay upfront, the cost can go onto a home loan, which is usually the cheapest money available to a homeowner. Some institutions also offer asset-backed solar finance to people without a bond, secured against the system itself.

Between the four routes, the question stops being whether you can afford solar and becomes which structure suits your situation. That's a conversation rather than a calculator, and it's part of what a site visit is for.
