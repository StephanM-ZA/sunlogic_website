What gas is actually good at

Three things, and they are worth being specific about rather than vague.

Instant heat you can see. A gas flame responds the moment you turn it, and it goes down as fast as it goes up. It is why professional kitchens use it, and it is the reason most people who cook seriously want it.

Hot water that does not run out. An instant gas water heater heats water as it passes through, so there is no tank to empty and nothing keeping forty litres warm all night for the sake of one shower in the morning.

It works when the power does not. Faults, storms and planned municipal maintenance all take the lights out, and a gas hob carries on through all of them. An instant gas water heater does too, depending on the model: some need a little electricity for ignition and controls, so tell us it matters and we will specify one that does not. It is a quieter benefit than it sounds until the first time you need it.

Flue or no flue decides most of the job

This is where the standard draws its own line, and it is not the one people expect. A hob or a small sink heater in a properly ventilated kitchen is one kind of job. A bath-size water heater, a shower or a high-output heater has to vent to outside air, and that changes where it can go, what the room needs and what it costs.

Vent-free heaters are a third case again: no flue, but the room has to be big enough for the appliance, and bedrooms and bathrooms have rules of their own. We work this out on site, because it decides the position of everything else.

 Why a cylinder goes cold halfway through a shower. It is almost never the appliance. A cylinder does not just hold gas, it has to boil it, and how fast it can do that depends on the size of the cylinder, how much liquid is left in it and how cold the morning is. Draw gas faster than the cylinder can vaporise it and the pressure drops, the cylinder frosts over and the flame dies, with gas still in the bottle. What usually goes wrong

Two things, and neither of them is the equipment.

The first is storage. The standard limits how much gas may be kept inside a building, and for most commercial kitchens that limit means the supply cannot be kept indoors at all. The supply goes outside, with clearances from openings, drains and the boundary, and on a tight city site, finding a compliant position is the hardest part of the whole job.

The second is approvals. In the City of Cape Town, a gas installation over 100 kg, or under 100 kg where the pipe is embedded in the concrete floor, needs approved plans and a flammable substance certificate application to the Fire and Rescue Service. Requirements differ by municipality, so Stellenbosch, Drakenstein and the rest are their own conversation, and we check yours rather than assuming.

A restaurant with a wood-fired or commercial oven usually needs a second, separate application as well, under air quality rules. Different department, different timeline, and it is the one that catches people out when they have already signed a lease and set an opening date.

Where the job stops being ours alone

An individual vessel over 500 litres moves the installation into a different part of the standard, with a different scope of work. If your site is heading that way we will tell you early, because it changes the design, the siting and the programme rather than just the price.

And whatever the size, the standard's own instruction is that the fire department be brought in early when gas storage is being planned. Early, not at inspection. That is the single biggest difference between a commissioning date that holds and one that slips.

What we actually do

Staged pressure testing, each stage held and watched for a drop. A regulator performance check taken three ways: everything alight, the smallest burner alight, and everything off. Pipe sizing checked under full load at the furthest and highest-consumption burner, because a system can be fine with one appliance running and starved with three.

Then corrosion, right through the pipework, valves, relief valve, regulator and cylinder condition. Ventilation checked, including that the vents cannot be blocked, which is the most common real-world failure and the easiest one to cause by accident. And consumables replaced on age rather than on appearance, because a hose that looks fine and is past its service life is still past its service life.

You get the readings in writing and the next date. That is the whole point of it.

Why we shorten the interval at the coast

The standard sets a maximum interval and then says that where conditions demand it, the period should be reduced. Its own service checklist comes back to corrosion again and again, and its siting rules tell installers to keep clear of corrosive conditions. Salt air off the Atlantic is a condition that demands it. On a seafront property we service more often than the maximum, and we put the reason in writing rather than just charging for more visits.

We service installations we did not put in

Most of what we service was installed by somebody else. We will tell you what we find before we touch anything, including when the honest answer is that something needs replacing rather than servicing.

 A certificate is not a service, and it does not expire on a date. A Certificate of Conformity records that an installation conformed on the day it was issued. Nothing in it maintains your hoses. There is no validity period, renewal interval or expiry anywhere in the regulation, and the certificate form the regulation prescribes has no expiry field and nowhere to put one. What is true instead is that a fresh certificate is required when the installation changes or the property changes hands, and that the installation itself should be re-inspected periodically. If someone tells you your certificate expired on a particular date, ask them which regulation says so.

A gas certificate is required when ownership changes. That is the regulation's own trigger, not your attorney's preference, and conveyancers will not proceed to transfer without one.

Get it before the offer rather than during the transfer. An installation that needs work is a negotiation when you have time, and a problem when you do not.

Ask what the certificate covers, because it covers the installation and not the appliances, and it was true on the day it was signed.

If the property has gas and nobody can produce a certificate, that is something to price in rather than something to assume is fine. We will inspect it for you before you commit.
