The headline is the weakest line on the page

Hero · live

"Solar That Pays For Itself"
Issue

Every solar company in the country says this. It is the category's default sentence, which means it identifies you as one of them at the exact moment you are trying to be the other thing. It also makes a payback promise the next two thousand words spend their time qualifying, including a call-out warning readers about proposals that promise fast payback. The page argues against its own headline.

Hero · the document's version

"Solar Sized On Your Bill"
Also no

Better, because it points at the mechanism. But the home page now says we size on what you use, not your roof, and it deliberately dropped bill language to stop sounding residential. Two pages, one click apart, arguing in different vocabularies.

Fix

The real idea is already in the WORTH KNOWING block directly underneath: a backup system and a bill system are different systems, and most people are quoted the wrong one. Nobody else in this market opens with that. Four ways to say it:

A
Most Quotes Size the Wrong System

Leads with the mistake. Makes a reader with two quotes in hand want to check them.

B
Sized On What You Use, Not What You Fear

The mechanism and the mistake in one line, and it matches the home page's vocabulary exactly.

C
Built to Cut a Bill, Not to Survive a Blackout

Most concrete. Names both systems so the reader knows immediately which one they were sold.

D
Two Houses, One Street, Two Different Systems

Curiosity rather than claim. Weakest of the four commercially, strongest at getting the next line read.

Why it matters: A headline everybody uses is not a neutral choice, it is an active one. It spends your first impression telling the reader you are the same as the last two quotes they got.

Line level

Business · the demand charge, twice

Issue, and this one is mine

I added a call-out to the business section this week saying you are billed on your highest demand of the month. The larger sites section already says the same thing, better, in It Will Barely Touch. Same fact, two framings, twelve hundred words apart, and the second one quietly contradicts the first by explaining that solar does not help with it.

Fix

One of them goes. Keep the larger sites version, which is honest about what solar cannot do. The business section gets the point it is missing instead, which is that a smaller business on a simple tariff may not have a demand charge at all, and that is worth knowing before they read the larger sites section and worry.

Ways to pay · four routes

Buy it outright · Finance it · Rent to own · Let someone else own it
Issue

These are live, presented as things a reader can choose. The copy document's own note beside them says: confirm which of these Sunlogic actually offers or has finance partners for, because advertising one you cannot deliver costs the enquiry. That note did not make it onto the page. The same applies to Paying For It in the home section.

Fix

Not a writing job. Mark each of the four as offered, referred, or neither, and cut what is neither. A reader who picks the fourth one and finds out on the phone that it is not really available has been sold something twice and got neither.

Why it matters: This is the one section on a page full of honesty where the page might not be able to keep its word.

Home · common questions · registration

"At the time of writing there is no registration or connection fee, though that is due to change at the end of September 2026."
Issue

That is this month. In a few weeks this sentence is either wrong or stale, and it is buried inside an accordion where nobody will remember it lives.

Fix

Two things. Put a review date on it somewhere you will actually see, and consider that a fee arriving at the end of September is a reason to move now. Right now the page treats a deadline as a footnote. It is the only genuine piece of urgency you have, and it is true, which is rarer than it sounds.

Structure · three audiences, one scroll

Issue

A homeowner scrolls past a business calculator, four ways for a company to finance a system, and a section about demand charges on factory tariffs, to reach a closing call to action written for all three. The sub nav helps, but only for a reader who notices it and knows which of the three they are.

Fix

Close each audience where it ends, rather than once at the foot. A homeowner who has just read about battery guarantees is closer to booking than the same person eleven hundred words later. The single close at the end is written for whoever is left, and the people left are the ones who were already going to call.

Why it matters: Ask for the sale where the argument lands, not where the page runs out.

Business · shape

Issue

What it costs, one thing to check early, common questions. The home section above it is: what will it save, do I need a battery, will it fit, what does it cost, common questions. The business section reads as a shorter copy of the home one rather than a different argument for a different reader.

Fix

The business reader's questions are not smaller versions of a homeowner's. They are: what does it do to my cost per unit, who signs it off, what happens to trading while you install, and what does my accountant need. Three of those are somewhere on the page. Order them the way a business owner would ask them and the section stops feeling like an afterthought.

Larger sites · What solar will and will not fix

"It will barely touch the demand charge, which is based on your highest peak in the month. On most sites that peak lands on a winter evening, when there is no sun."
Working, and it is the best thing here

Two cards, one saying what you get and one saying what you do not. It is specific, it is checkable against their own invoice, and no competitor's proposal will contain it. If one block on this page had to survive a rewrite, it is this pair.

The verdict

This page is a long way ahead of where the home page started. It has a real argument, it answers questions in the order people ask them, and its instinct to give things away is the most commercially valuable thing on the whole site. It is held back by a headline any of your competitors could have written and by one section it may not be able to deliver.

Three changes

  1. Replace the hero. The idea is sitting in the block directly beneath it. Four options above; the page cannot go out arguing against its own headline.
  2. Resolve the four ways to pay. Offered, referred, or gone. This is the only place on the page where you might not be able to keep your word.
  3. Close each audience where it ends. One close at the foot of a 2,300 word page is a close for people who had already decided.

Do not let anything change these

The tariff call-out. "Selling power back can put you on a tariff that costs you more." The equivalent of the fixed charge line on the home page: checkable, surprising, and against your own interest. Best block on the page.

"There is a real judgement call in there, and it is one we would rather make with you than for you." That sentence is the entire company in nineteen words.

"Anyone telling you otherwise has done something to the arithmetic." Says the quiet part, and a reader holding an oversold proposal will recognise themselves immediately.

It Will Reduce and It Will Barely Touch. Keep them as a pair. Separately they are facts. Together they are a demonstration that you will tell people what solar cannot do.

"We work it out from your last twelve months of bills rather than guessing from your roof." Same mechanism as the home page, same words. Consistency across two pages is worth more than a better sentence on one of them.

Verify before this ships

  • The four ways to pay. Which are real, which are referrals, which are neither.
  • The home finance list. Same question, same paragraph problem.
  • The registration fee date. End of September 2026 is now. Needs an owner and a review date.
  • Both tax paragraphs. They already say confirm with your accountant, which is right, but the enhanced allowance claim needs a date check.

Cut or move

  • The demand charge call-out in the business section. Duplicate. The larger sites version is better and more honest.
  • "Solar That Pays For Itself." The page spends two thousand words disagreeing with it.
  • The single closing call to action. Not cut, multiplied. One per audience, where that audience's argument ends.
