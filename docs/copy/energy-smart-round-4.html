Nothing left that better writing fixes. What remains is missing information: two of the three pillars still cannot tell a reader what they cost.

Question one: monitoring is 597 words, hot water is 344

It is a defensible choice, and I would keep it. The page was making a different claim than I thought.

PillarWordsWhy
hot water344Two products with specifications. A reader gets it fast
switching347A familiar object doing an unfamiliar job
monitoring597No category the reader already has. Everything has to be built
AI332Sells nothing. Positions everything
smart home58Waiting on a brand list

Word count follows how hard something is to explain, not how much it matters. HotBot is a box with a price and a job; VoltIQ is a platform nobody has heard of that produces a document nobody has seen. Cutting it to match hot water would make it worse.

The sentence was the problem, not the sections. "The order that pays" reads as a ranking of importance, and then the page contradicts it with its own layout. It now says "the order to spend in", and adds one line: monitoring gets the most space because it is the one nobody has heard of, not because it comes last. That is the whole fix, and it retires a tension three rounds kept circling.

Question two: smart home is 58 words and mostly a placeholder

It is not earning its place commercially, and cutting it would still be wrong.

The page opens by arguing that a lot of smart home is convenience dressed as a saving. Make that argument and then never mention smart home again and you have attacked a category and offered the reader nothing. The section exists to close the loop the thesis opens: yes we do it, no it is not where the saving is, and we would rather say so.

So it earns its place argumentatively while being commercially empty. Fifty eight honest words beat two hundred that oversell. The fix is the brand list, not the word count. That list turns this from a placeholder into the detail that decides who somebody phones.

Worth noticing
AI section332 words, sells nothing
Smart home58 words, is a service you offer

The page currently spends six times more words disclaiming AI than describing a thing it will actually install for you. The AI section earns its length because it is the most quotable material on the site and it inoculates against every competitor's claims. But that ratio is only defensible while the brand list is missing. Once it lands, smart home should grow.

Question three: is the page finished

As copy, yes. There is nothing left that better writing improves. The thesis is clear and differentiated, each pillar makes its own argument, the honesty sections do real competitive work, and the page now shows two genuine documents rather than describing them.

Commercially, no, and the gap is specific.

PillarWhat a reader can find out
hot waterR849 once off, R149 a month, R99 for extra units
switchingnothing
monitoring"depending on your subscription"

The one thing you resell has exact figures. The two things you own and build have none. A reader who finishes this page convinced that switching is the right next step cannot find out what it would cost them, and that is the single biggest thing standing between this page and a booking.

What round four actually changed

  • One line added to the order callout, and its label changed from "The order that pays" to "The order to spend in".

That is all, and it is the right amount for a fourth round. When a review starts proposing rewrites of copy that is already working, it has run out of findings and started manufacturing them.

Carried forward from earlier rounds

  • Round three caught a callout headed "What you actually get" sitting directly above a card headed "What You Get", answering two different questions with the same words. The cards are "Written for You" and "Written for Us" now.
  • Introducing real screenshots changed what the captions had to do. They were narrating the picture: the report's own headline says the panels are fine, and the caption said it again. Rewritten to carry what the reader cannot see, which is who this was and what they had assumed.
  • The captions assumed a gender. "He", "his", "him", about a client whose gender is not known and did not need to be. Rewritten through direct-response-copy with that as a hard constraint, and verified: zero gendered pronouns remain on the page.
  • The redaction disclaimer came off at the owner's instruction. The redaction itself stands: name, street address and station ID were removed before the screenshots were taken, and nothing else was altered.
