# Gas sells compliance in Cape Town, not savings

South African gas work is a compliance trade wearing the costume of an energy trade, and that is the single most important finding for Sunlogic Energy's website. The law — regulation 17 of the Pressure Equipment Regulations, 2009 under the Occupational Health and Safety Act 85 of 1993 — prohibits anyone but an "authorised person" from installing or removing a gas appliance, and requires a Certificate of Conformity on installation, modification, alteration **or change of user or ownership** ([Pressure Equipment Regulations reg 17](https://source.acts.co.za/occupational-health-and-safety-act-1993/per_nr734_17_gas_reticulation_equipment_and_systems.php)). That change-of-ownership trigger, enforced in practice by conveyancers rather than by the Deeds Office, is the most reliable demand stream in the market. The energy case is narrower than the industry pretends: at the **regulated Western Cape maximum of R37.08/kg (7 October – 3 November 2026)** LPG beats a City of Cape Town electric element geyser by roughly **25–40% per useful kilowatt-hour**, ties Eskom Homelight 60A, and **loses outright** to Eskom Homelight 20A, the CoCT Lifeline tariff, solar water heating and a heat pump — and gas **cooking is more expensive than electric cooking** at Cape Town tariffs. Load-shedding is dead as a selling point: South Africa passed **504 consecutive days without Eskom load-shedding** by early October 2026. The competitive opening is unusually clean: of seven Western Cape competitor sites read in full, **none sells a scheduled servicing product, none publishes a CoC price, five publish no SAQCC registration number, and not one frames "one contractor, one certificate trail"** — the exact position a solar and electrical contractor is already half-way into. The binding constraint on writing this content is verification: almost every SANS distance, clearance, ventilation and bulk-tank figure in South African gas marketing traces to secondary blogs or to the **superseded 2008 Edition 5** of SANS 10087-1, and the current edition (Edition 7, 2024) is paywalled and its changes unknown.

## The law names an "authorised person" — SAQCC Gas is how that gets administered, not what the regulation says

The chain is short and worth getting exactly right, because the compressed version that circulates in the market is subtly wrong in a way a well-informed customer can catch. Regulation 17(1)(c) of the Pressure Equipment Regulations, 2009 (Government Notice R.734, 15 July 2009) prohibits any person from installing or removing "a gas appliance, gas system, or gas reticulation system unless the person is an 'authorised person'", and regulation 1 defines an authorised person, verbatim, as **"a person who is registered as competent within the scope of work"** ([PER reg 1](https://source.acts.co.za/occupational-health-and-safety-act-1993/per_nr734_1_definitions.php); [PER reg 17](https://source.acts.co.za/occupational-health-and-safety-act-1993/per_nr734_17_gas_reticulation_equipment_and_systems.php)). **SAQCC Gas appears nowhere in the regulation.** It derives its authority from a mandate by the Department of Employment and Labour, and describes itself as registering gas practitioners on that Department's behalf across four industries — Natural Gas, Liquefied Petroleum Gas, Air Conditioning and Refrigeration Gas, and Compressed Industrial and Medical Gases ([saqccgas.co.za](https://www.saqccgas.co.za/)). The correct formulation for copy is therefore: *the law requires an authorised person, registered as competent within the scope of work; the Department of Employment and Labour has mandated SAQCC Gas to do that registration, so in practice it means a currently-registered SAQCC Gas practitioner holding the right category for the work.*

Two things follow that are commercially useful. First, the words **"within the scope of work"** are what give legal force to category limits — a practitioner registered for domestic LPG who signs off a commercial reticulation job is arguably not an authorised person *for that work*, which is the sharpest compliance argument available to a properly-registered contractor. Second, registration is **perishable**: a gas practitioner is issued a three-year licence, renewable on proof of ongoing competence ([Mining Weekly](https://www.miningweekly.com/article/how-gas-compliance-works-during-covid-19-2020-06-24)), and the physical card shows the holder's scope and the card's expiry date ([Engineering News, 29 August 2024](https://www.engineeringnews.co.za/article/safety-tips-for-lpg-cylinders-for-heating-and-cooking-2024-08-29)). Any claim of registration should therefore be paired with a verifiable number, because SAQCC Gas publishes a searchable practitioner register with per-practitioner pages showing the SAQCC ID, employer, town and the expiry date of each gas skill category ([saqccgas.co.za member page](http://saqccgas.co.za/member/?r=8074)). That register is the single strongest trust asset in the niche and it is almost entirely unused by Cape Town competitors.

The Certificate of Conformity itself is created by regulation 17(3), must be in the form of Annexure 1, and is triggered by four events: **installation, modification, alteration, or change of user or ownership**. The prescribed form carries the authorised person's name, registration number and ID number, the installation address, the gas supplier, the gas type, **the mass of gas stored on the premises in kilograms**, a declaration that the person inspected and tested the installation, and the warning "I am aware that I am liable to prosecution in the case of a false declaration" ([PER Annexure 1](https://source.acts.co.za/occupational-health-and-safety-act-1993/per_nr734_annexure_1_certificate_of_conformity_for_gas_installations.php)). Separately, regulation 17(2) places the duty to arrange a pre-commissioning external inspection and leak test on the **user**, not the installer — a legitimate and well-sourced point for homeowner and business-owner copy: the obligation is theirs, not just their plumber's.

Since 2021 the CoC has moved to a compulsory electronic format. The electronic system launched 1 March 2021 ([Mining Weekly, 18 May 2021](https://www.miningweekly.com/article/moving-into-the-digital-space-electronic-cocs-2021-05-18)); the app became compulsory in September 2022 ([Engineering News, 9 September 2022](https://www.engineeringnews.co.za/article/electronic-certificate-of-conformity-now-compulsory-2022-09-09)); and from **1 April 2023 a paper-issued CoC is no longer valid** ([Mining Weekly, 7 June 2023](https://www.miningweekly.com/article/saqcc-gas-discusses-new-electronic-cocs-at-securex-2023-2023-06-07)). From **1 May 2023 all gas CoCs must be in the prescribed five-page form**, gas compliance companies may not use any other format, and conveyancers will not accept any other format for a property transfer ([Property Professional, 3 July 2023](https://propertyprofessional.co.za/2023/07/03/a-new-gas-coc-is-now-required/); [MyProperty](https://myproperty.co.za/faq/legal/new-gas-compliance-certificates)). This is a genuine differentiator worth writing about: because the certificate is issued only through the SAQCC Gas portal, its authenticity is independently checkable, and a customer handed a paper or improvised PDF certificate should be suspicious.

The property-transfer question needs care, because the market overstates it. Regulation 17(3) does create a real obligation to **issue** a CoC on change of ownership — that part is not a myth. But on the text available, the regulation does not make the transfer itself unlawful or make Deeds Office registration conditional on a certificate; the requirement bites because conveyancers and standard sale agreements make it a condition, and because the five-page prescribed form is the only one they will accept. Sellers normally obtain it, though the parties can agree otherwise, and a failed inspection means the seller carries repair costs under transfer-date pressure ([Rawson](https://blog.rawson.co.za/what-documents-and-compliance-certificates-do-sellers-need); [STBB](https://stbb.co.za/thought-of-the-week-gas-certificate-of-conformity/)). The requirement is **national** — the PER applies uniformly and no source suggests the CoC-on-transfer trigger differs by province.

Commercial work adds two municipal layers on top of the national one. In the City of Cape Town, **any LPG installation over 100 kg — and any installation under 100 kg where the pipe is embedded in a concrete floor — requires a Flammable Substance Certificate with prescribed plans submitted to Fire and Rescue via Building Development Management**, with site plans at 1:1000 to 1:100, layout plans at 1:100/1:50/1:20, drawings signed by the owner, and layout drawings showing pipework and distances to other flammable installations, wall openings, drains and flues, regulator and safety valve positions and extinguisher locations ([City of Cape Town, Gas requirements](https://resource.capetown.gov.za/documentcentre/Documents/Procedures%2c%20guidelines%20and%20regulations/Gas_requirements.pdf) — **note: the City's server refused connection twice, so this content is search-extracted and must be re-read before publication**). The underlying instrument is the Community Fire Safety By-law, 2002 ([Lawlibrary](https://lawlibrary.org.za/akn/za-cpt/act/by-law/2002/community-fire-safety)). Separately, commercial pizza ovens, braais and fireplaces go through an **Air Quality Management By-law application to a different City department**, not the fire-safety route ([City of Cape Town fuel-burning equipment application](https://resource.capetown.gov.za/documentcentre/Documents/Forms,%20notices,%20tariffs%20and%20lists/Application%20to%20install%20fuel%20burning%20equipment.pdf)). A restaurant with both a gas reticulation system and a wood-fired oven therefore needs **two separate municipal applications to two different departments** — a specific, verifiable, and completely unsold piece of knowledge. At bulk quantities the national Major Hazard Installation Regulations, 2022 (Government Notice R2989 of 2023) also engage, tiering establishments as lower, medium and high hazard against qualifying quantities in Annexure A ([Lawlibrary](https://lawlibrary.org.za/akn/za/act/gn/2023/r2989/eng@2023-01-31)) — **but the LPG qualifying quantity could not be obtained and must not be guessed.**

On penalties, be honest and don't lean on the statute. Contravention of regulations 3 to 19 is an offence carrying a fine or imprisonment up to **12 months**, plus **R200 per day** for a continuing offence with additional imprisonment capped at 90 days ([PER reg 20](https://source.acts.co.za/occupational-health-and-safety-act-1993/per_nr734_20_offences_and_penalties.php)). The R200/day figure has evidently not been uprated since 2009 and is trivial in 2026 terms. The insurance argument is real but weaker than the market claims: insurers and property publishers state that a claim for gas-related damage **can** be declined where the installation was not done by a registered installer and there is no certificate ([Property24](https://www.property24.com/articles/how-to-safely-and-legally-install-gas-appliances-in-your-home/31165); [Private Property](https://www.privateproperty.co.za/advice/property/articles/gas-compliance-required-for-insurance/9490)), and Santam's Head of Non-Motor Claims gives risk-management advice around gas CoCs ([Insurance Biz, 30 March 2022](https://insurancebiz.co.za/news/non-life/with-outages-expected-every-thrid-day,-is-it-time-to-get-gas.html)) — **but no South African ombudsman determination, reported judgment, or policy wording making a gas CoC a condition precedent to cover was found.** The strongest *verifiable* consequence chain is transactional, not criminal and not insurance: no CoC in the prescribed form → conveyancer will not proceed → transfer stalls → seller carries repair costs against a deadline. Write that one.

## The gas service has hard numbers in it, and almost nobody in Cape Town publishes them

The most valuable technical finding across the notes is that **SANS 10087-1 defines what a gas service is far more precisely than any Cape Town installer website does** — and the full text of **Edition 5 (2008)** is publicly readable on archive.org, which is where essentially every hard figure below comes from ([SANS 10087-1:2008](https://archive.org/download/za.sans.10087.1.2008/za.sans.10087.1.2008.pdf)). The current edition is **Edition 7, approved August 2024**, superseding **SANS 10087-1:2013 (Edition 6)** ([SAIAT CPD](https://cpd.saiat.org.za/lp-gas-installations)). **No clause-by-clause list of 2024 changes could be obtained**, so every figure that follows is verified against a superseded edition and must be re-checked against Edition 7 before it goes on a page. That caveat is not a formality: it is the difference between competent copy and a liability.

Within that caveat, the service content is specific. Installations are tested in sequence — high pressure, intermediate pressure where used, then low pressure. The twin-cylinder high-pressure method uses a 0–600 kPa gauge with a dial at least 100 mm, five minutes to equalise temperature, then **a further ten minutes with no pressure drop**. The intermediate stage is tested with inert gas, air or nitrogen at **200 kPa for a minimum of 10 minutes**. The low-pressure U-gauge method allows ten minutes to stabilise and then ten minutes more, where **a drop of more than 25 mm water gauge means there is a leak**. Buried or embedded pipework in critical locations is tested at **at least 400 kPa held for at least 15 minutes**, must be welded by a competent welder, and may contain no mechanical joints. The regulator performance check uses a water manometer and three numerical limits: pressure must **not fall below 2.7 kPa** with all burners alight, **not exceed 3.4 kPa** with only the smallest burner alight, and **not exceed 4.0 kPa** with all burners off. Pipe sizing is adequate only if pressure stays **at or above 2.5 kPa** at the highest-consumption and farthest burners under full load. And consumables carry hard ages: **hoses and pigtails replaced at five years** (automatically if UV-exposed, and for outdoor installations five years after installation), **hydrostatic relief valves replaced at over five years**, **regulators replaced or refurbished at over seven years**, all inside a whole-installation re-inspection interval **not exceeding five years** ([SANS 10087-1:2008](https://archive.org/download/za.sans.10087.1.2008/za.sans.10087.1.2008.pdf)).

That five-year interval is the real origin of the market's "your CoC is valid for five years" claim, and it is the key to writing about servicing honestly. There is **no statutory expiry for a gas CoC** — the PER text prescribes none. What exists is clause 10.1's five-year maximum re-inspection interval plus the five-year automatic replacement of outdoor hoses, which together mean an installation cannot be assumed leak-free on original consumables after five years. **"Re-inspection and re-certification every five years" is accurate; "your CoC expires after five years" is not.** And the standard itself invites a shorter interval: the manifold inspection note says *"If conditions demand, this period should be reduced"*, while the service checklist centres corrosion repeatedly — pipework, valves, relief valve, regulator, cylinder condition — and siting rules tell installers to avoid positions near corrosive substances. **For a seafront or coastal Cape Town property that is a sourced, non-marketing argument for a two- or three-year service interval rather than riding the five-year maximum.** It is also the cleanest available justification for the recurring servicing product that no competitor sells.

The best organising idea for a residential services page is **flue or no flue**, because that is where the standard actually draws its line. Gas stoves, hotplates and sink water heaters in kitchens may be exempt from flueing if used at maximum rating for short periods only, provided the kitchen has at least one airbrick in an external wall with a ventilating area of **at least 160 cm²**. Appliances with a high consumption rate or long operating periods — **multipoint and bath-size water heaters, showers and high-output space heaters** — must have flues to outside air. Vent-free heaters need no flue but trigger a hard room-volume calculation: if total heat input of the vent-free heater plus any other gas, solid-fuel or paraffin appliance in the room exceeds **0.37 MJ per m³ of room volume**, permanent ventilation is required, and the standard's own worked example is a 25 MJ appliance needing 25 ÷ 0.37 = **67.57 m³**. Vent-free heaters in bedrooms **always** need permanent ventilation regardless of room size, and **vent-free space heaters may not be installed in separately enclosed bathrooms**. Conventional gas geysers in bathrooms must be flued to outside with permanent ventilation, and the standard carries a standing warning about high gas consumption in small bathrooms where users minimise air entry ([SANS 10087-1:2008](https://archive.org/download/za.sans.10087.1.2008/za.sans.10087.1.2008.pdf)). General ventilation sizing is two vents, high and low, with total free cross-sectional area of **not less than 13 cm² per MJ** of total rated heat input, where free area means the sum of the open holes and not the vent's outside dimensions, and **"vents shall be installed in such a way that they cannot be blocked"** — the single most common real-world ventilation failure.

Then there is the one clause that makes a solar and electrical contractor structurally better placed than a gas-only installer. Clause 9.1 requires that **electrical equipment such as light switches and plugs be at least 200 mm from any burner or potential point of gas release**; that where a gas hob is installed together with an electric oven **a three-point plug socket shall not be used** to connect the oven; that **a proper isolator switch be fitted above hob level with at least 200 mm clearance**; and that **no electrical connection be made below the hob**. Gas piping must also be **at least 150 mm from parallel electrical cables**, may cross them only without contact and with no joints within 150 mm of the crossing, and may not share a service with electrical bus-bars. Where electrical equipment falls inside the gas safety distance, SANS 10087-3 hazardous-area zone requirements apply ([SANS 10087-1:2008](https://archive.org/download/za.sans.10087.1.2008/za.sans.10087.1.2008.pdf)). A typical "put a gas hob in with an electric oven" job therefore contains a real electrical scope — isolator, hard-wired oven circuit, clearances — and the market's gas installers either subcontract it or do it informally.

Cylinder sizing is where the standard supports a genuinely differentiating customer explanation. **Vaporisation rate, not capacity, is the failure mode.** The standard's Table 1 caps approximate vaporisation at **230 g/h for a 9 kg cylinder, 460 g/h for 19 kg, 920 g/h for 48 kg and 2,300 g/h for a 196 kg (454 L) container**, and explains that off-take diminishes until it reaches equilibrium with the vaporisation rate, which depends on container size, remaining LPG and **the lowest expected ambient temperature**. The cylinder-count table is explicitly "based on results that are typical for cold-winter conditions in South Africa; in warmer conditions the requirements will be less" — on that table a multipoint instantaneous water heater needs **4.60 × 9 kg, 2.00 × 19 kg or exactly 1.00 × 48 kg** cylinders. The standard's own default recommendation for an ordinary permanent dwelling is **one 48 kg supply cylinder plus one 48 kg reserve on changeover** ([SANS 10087-1:2008](https://archive.org/download/za.sans.10087.1.2008/za.sans.10087.1.2008.pdf)). This is the technical cause of the "cylinder frosts over and the flame dies mid-shower in winter" complaint: it is a **sizing fault, not an appliance fault**, and it is the most satisfying thing in the whole technical pack to explain to a customer. For Cape Town specifically, the standard invites local adjustment — milder winters than the Highveld mean the national rule of thumb is conservative here, in either direction, and saying so explicitly is better than copying Gauteng guidance.

The commercial decision tree falls out of the same clauses in a sequence a contractor can present as a diagnostic. Indoor mass limits are **9 kg per flat (H3)**, **19 kg total in a house** including cluster and group housing up to two storeys, **19 kg per unit in commercial occupancies** with a SANS 10400-compliant separating element, and **19 kg per 600 m³ with a 100 kg total maximum in industrial premises (D1–D4)**. So a restaurant cannot keep its supply inside — cylinders go outside, more than two cylinders triggers manifolding (a changeover device on one container each side is explicitly **not** a manifold, though the safety distances still apply), manifolds cap at **ten containers per branch and 20 cylinders per installation at ≤48 kg** or **three per branch and six per installation at ≤196 kg**, and an individual vessel over **500 L water capacity** moves the job out of Part 1 and into **SANS 10087-3**. Above **100 kg combined capacity** the standard requires a **9 kg dry powder extinguisher to SANS 1910** near the installation, and the standard separately instructs that **"the local fire department shall be consulted at an early stage when LPG storage is planned on a site"** ([SANS 10087-1:2008](https://archive.org/download/za.sans.10087.1.2008/za.sans.10087.1.2008.pdf)). That 100 kg figure is roughly five or six 19 kg cylinders — present it as arithmetic, not as a quoted rule — and it lines up with the City of Cape Town's own FSC trigger, which is why most restaurants with gas cooking will exceed it.

Two Cape Town context points matter for scoping. First, **the Western Cape has no reticulated natural gas at all** — the only natural-gas item found for the province is a Western Cape Government **pre-feasibility study** on importing gas via the Saldanha Bay–Cape Town corridor ([WCG study](https://stocks.metabolismofcities.org/library/43523/download/43526)). Cape Town gas work is LPG work, full stop, and "natural gas conversion" is a non-service here. The honest inversion is the useful one: appliances arriving set up for natural gas — imports, relocations from Gauteng or KZN, second-hand equipment from a Highveld restaurant — must be **converted to LPG**, which means **smaller injectors**, re-set simmer screws, the right regulator pressure and a new data plate ([Gorenje conversion instruction](https://partners.gorenje.com/fts/htmlNavodila/567972en.html); direction-of-change evidence that LPG jets are smaller than natural-gas jets at [Electricians Forum](https://electricianforum.co.uk/threads/gas-hob-jet-conversion-for-lpg.37894)). Note that **all conversion-kit sourcing and pricing evidence is UK/EU/AU — no South African distributor or price was found.** Second, the national "gas cliff" is a tailwind rather than a threat: Sasol's third-party industrial gas supply to Mpumalanga, Gauteng and KZN is winding down, with the operative date having moved from end-June 2026 to around **July 2028**, methane-rich gas from Secunda proposed as a bridge from July 2028 to June 2030, roughly **300 industrial gas buyers** affected and job-loss estimates of **70,000 to 100,000** ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/south-africa-will-run-out-of-industrial-gas-by-2028-and-70-000-jobs-could-be-lost-why-government-fixes-are-lacking); [Mining Weekly, 23 September 2026](https://www.miningweekly.com/article/south-africa-urged-to-take-action-as-the-countrys-gas-reserves-decline-2026-09-23)). None of that touches Western Cape households, and **it should not be used as a Cape Town customer driver** — but it does mean LPG and on-site storage are the near-term substitutes nationally.

## LPG beats a Cape Town electric geyser by 25–40% and loses to almost everything else

This is the section where overclaiming will cost Sunlogic credibility, so take the numbers exactly as they are. LPG sold to domestic customers is subject to a **regulated maximum retail price per kilogram**, set monthly by the Department of Mineral and Petroleum Resources and gazetted by magisterial district zone. For **7 October to 3 November 2026** the maximum retail price is **R34.56/kg at the coast (Zone 1A)**, **R37.81/kg inland (Zone 9C, Gauteng)** and **R37.08/kg for LPG imported through Saldanha Bay** — which is the Western Cape price, because the province is served almost entirely by the Saldanha import terminal ([CEF/DMPR media statement, 2 October 2026](https://cefgroup.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Press-Release-02-October-2026-Change-07-October-2026.pdf)). Using the generic coastal figure would understate Cape Town's cost by 7.3%. The price is a published, auditable build-up — refinery gate price, primary transport, operating expenses, working capital, depreciation, cylinder-filling plant margin, then a **fixed 15% retail margin and 15% VAT on top** — which is the best possible defence against an accusation of cherry-picking, and worth saying: the commodity itself is only about **39–42%** of what a household pays per kilogram (researcher's own arithmetic on the gazetted elements).

The honest core of the gas argument is a **price-trajectory divergence**, not load-shedding and not an absolute saving. **Coastal LPG was 3,457 c/kg in January 2024 and 3,456 c/kg in October 2026 — a nominal change of −0.03% over 33 months**, which against South African CPI is a meaningful real decline ([CEF/DMPR Annexure 1](https://cefgroup.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Press-Release-02-October-2026-Change-07-October-2026.pdf); cross-confirmed by the [Fuels Industry Association price table](https://fuelsindustry.org.za/consumer-information/fuel-prices-current-past/)). Over the same window electricity rose **12.74% from 1 April 2025 and 8.76% from 1 April 2026** for Eskom direct customers (**9.01%** for municipalities from 1 July 2026), a compounded **+22.6% in 24 months**, with **8.83% already approved for 1 April 2027** ([Eskom Tariffs and Charges Booklet 2026/2027](https://www.eskom.co.za/distribution/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Eskom-Tariff-booklet_Final.pdf); [NERSA media statement, March 2026](https://www.nersa.org.za/files/files/2026/03/MediaStatement-NERSAapprovesEskomRetailTariffsandStructuralAdjustmentapplicationfor2026-27.pdf)). The 2026/27 increase was revised *upward* from an originally approved 5.36% after NERSA admitted calculation errors in depreciation and the Regulatory Asset Base that understated Eskom's allowable revenue by **R54.7 billion**, of which **R12 billion is recovered in 2026/27 and R23 billion in later years** ([Engineering News, 10 March 2026](https://www.engineeringnews.co.za/article/nersa-approves-higher-electricity-hikes-for-202627-after-r547bn-error-2026-03-10)). That is a documented, named reason to expect further above-inflation increases — a far better argument than any claim about outages.

Eskom rates are primary from the [tariff booklet](https://www.eskom.co.za/distribution/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Eskom-Tariff-booklet_Final.pdf); CoCT rates are secondary and **disputed** (see the conflicts section). Written as a monthly bill for a 200 L/day household (279 useful kWh/month): LPG at **R851–R963**, CoCT Domestic Block 1 electric at **R1,242**, Block 2 at **R1,490**, a heat pump at **R446**, and Eskom Homelight 20A at **R837** — where gas is more expensive. The only published South African comparison found puts an LP gas instant geyser at R700–R950/month and a standard electric geyser at R800–R1,000+ in winter 2026, with heat pump at R280–R330 and solar geyser at R200–R350 ([EnergyBee, 2 July 2026](https://energybee.co.za/news/cheapest-way-to-heat-water-south-africa-winter-2026)); the gas figures agree closely, the electric figure is materially lower than the tariff-traceable arithmetic, and that article is internally inconsistent on the point.

**Cooking goes the other way, and copy must not claim otherwise.** On standard pan-efficiency assumptions (gas 40%, radiant electric 70%, induction 84% — all unverified for South Africa) a **gas hob costs R6.68–R7.25 per useful kWh against radiant electric at R5.72–R6.87 and induction at R4.77–R5.72** at CoCT 2026/27 tariffs. That makes gas cooking roughly **17–27% more expensive than radiant electric and 25–50% more than induction**. The absolute amounts are small because cooking is a minor share of household energy, so this is not a reason to avoid gas — but **an electric-hob-to-gas-hob conversion has no energy payback at all** and must be sold on instant heat control, flame and wok cooking, working through an outage, and professional-kitchen preference. If actual gas pan efficiency is 50% rather than 40% the gap closes to roughly break-even with radiant electric; it still is not a saving.

Water-heating payback, stated conditionally, is the defensible claim: for a City of Cape Town household on the Domestic tariff using about 200 L/day, switching from an electric element geyser to an instant gas water heater saves roughly **R280–R640 a month** at October 2026 prices, paying back an **R8,000–R17,000** installation in **about one to five years, with roughly two years typical** (researcher's arithmetic). It **does not pay back** for low-usage households, for Eskom Homelight 20A or CoCT Lifeline customers, or where a heat pump is affordable — a heat pump at R27,500–R37,500 installed pays back in 26–36 months and then runs **cheaper than gas forever after** ([EnergyBee](https://energybee.co.za/news/cheapest-way-to-heat-water-south-africa-winter-2026)). Gas's genuine advantages over a heat pump are capital cost, instantaneous unlimited hot water, and operation without electricity — **not running cost**. There is also a structural drag worth disclosing: Eskom shifted the fixed portion of the Homepower service and administration charge from 33.3% to **66.6%** for FY2027 and raised the Generation Capacity Charge to 30% of RTP value, while CoCT Domestic carries **R74.77/month** and Home User **R424.30/month** regardless of consumption. **Gas reduces kilowatt-hours, not the connection charge** — so a 25–40% energy saving is not a 25–40% bill saving, and that gap widens every year.

Price risk belongs on the page too, because it is verifiable and disclosing it is more persuasive than hiding it. **Coastal LPG rose 508 c/kg (+15.5%) in a single adjustment between 1 April and 6 May 2026**, then fell 443 c/kg (−11.7%) between 1 July and 5 August 2026, with a **peak-to-trough spread of 22.9% inside seven months** (3,082 c/kg November 2025 to 3,787 c/kg May 2026). The driver is international: October 2026's increase was attributed to average Brent rising from **87.89 to 101 USD**, Strait of Hormuz uncertainty, higher shipping costs and falling inventories, with propane and butane specifically up on supply uncertainty ([Minister's media statement, 5 October 2026](https://www.gov.za/news/media-statements/minister-gwede-mantashe-announces-adjustment-fuel-prices-effective-7-0)). Unlike electricity, which steps once a year on an announced percentage, **LPG resets monthly in both directions and the household carries the risk.** Supply is import-dependent: domestic production fell from **235,000 t in 2020 to 123,000 t in 2024** while imports rose **more than 140% to 589,000 t** ([Argus Media](https://www.argusmedia.com/en/news-and-insights/latest-market-news/2757746-africa-s-lpg-growth-faces-supply-and-logistics-hurdles)), and **the Western Cape depends on a single terminal** — Sunrise Energy's **210,000 t/yr** facility at Saldanha Bay, described as the province's only LPG import terminal. Western Cape wholesalers have reported paying premiums above the regulated refinery gate price since EML took over as importer, with the department acknowledging Saldanha is more expensive than Richards Bay ([Argus Media](https://www.argusmedia.com/en/news-and-insights/latest-market-news/2636043-s-africa-eml-gets-2-yr-contract-at-sunrise-lpg-terminal), c. early 2025) — which is the structural explanation for the R37.08 versus R34.56 Western Cape premium.

The market context is favourable without needing invention. **29.0% of South African households use LPG or gas at all, and the Western Cape at 46.0% is the most gas-penetrated province in the country**, ahead of Gauteng at 36.3%; gas as a *primary* cooking fuel rose from **2.2% to 6.8%** of households across the General Household Survey series ([Stats SA, "The state of South African households in 2023"](https://www.statssa.gov.za/?p=17283); [Stats SA GHS 2024](https://www.statssa.gov.za/publications/P0318/P03182024.pdf)). That 46.0% figure is the single most useful market statistic for a Western Cape gas business. Policy points the same way: the LPG Rollout Strategy, gazetted for comment as Gazette 44384 and later approved by Cabinet, exists to expand household LPG use and reduce grid pressure ([DMRE draft LPG Rollout Strategy](https://www.dmre.gov.za/Portals/0/Energy_Website/files/policies/Gazette44384-for-comments-Draft-LPG-rollout-strategy.pdf)) — though **its reported "double the LPG market by 2026" target is now due and no official assessment of whether it was met was found, so do not claim it was.**

And the discipline that overrides everything in this section: **do not write "load-shedding" in the present tense.** South Africa reached a full year without a single second of load-shedding on 16 May 2026 — the first such run since September 2018 ([Eskom](https://www.eskom.co.za/eskom-delivers-365-days-without-loadshedding-advancing-the-economy-competition-and-the-integration-of-renewable-energy/)) — passed **504 consecutive days** by early October 2026 ([FurtherAfrica, 6 October 2026](https://furtherafrica.com/2026/10/06/eskom-load-shedding-ends-for-504-straight-days/)), came through winter 2026 load-shedding-free, and lifted its energy availability factor to **67.79%, the highest since 2020** ([SAnews](https://www.sanews.gov.za/south-africa/eskom-carries-south-africa-through-load-shedding-free-winter)). Residual targeted **load reduction** affects about 202,590 customers, 2.8% of Eskom's base, concentrated in Gauteng and KwaZulu-Natal, and barely touches the Western Cape. The honest resilience line is *"gas keeps working during a power outage"* — true for faults, storms and planned municipal maintenance, and supported by Stats SA's own observation that households turn to LPG during electrical interruptions. It is not a load-shedding claim.

## Seven Western Cape competitors, zero service plans: the positioning conclusion

The Western Cape field is small, sub-scale, and technically weak, and the gaps in it are documented rather than assumed. The national LPG suppliers have handed the entire lead-capture layer to local independents: **Easigas tells customers installation "must be carried out by an approved LP GAS engineer" and to phone its Customer Service Centre to find one**, with no public installer-locator page ([Easigas](https://www.easigas.co.za/phone/commercial--industrial-use.html)), and Afrox positions Handigas for supply with technical advice rather than a consumer install offer ([Afrox LP Gas](https://www.afrox.co.za/en/industrial/gas_products-and-suppliers/lp_gas/lp_gas_applications/lpg_application_technical_data/index.html)). No Western Cape-facing installer or installer-locator pages were found at all for Oryx Energies, TotalEnergies/Totalgaz, KayaGas or Avedia Energy. **There is no national brand soaking up household gas-install search demand in this province.**

Seven competitor sites were read in full in October 2026 and the pattern is consistent. Only **Gas Viper** publishes an SAQCC number (ID 5389) together with LPGSA membership and contractors' risk insurance — but its supporting LPGSASA certificate is dated **2019–2020**, its awards reference is from **2017**, it has **no H1 element and no structured data**, it does not state its service area anywhere, and its homepage service cards link to the wrong pages ([gasviper.co.za](https://gasviper.co.za/)). **Cape Gas Installations** publishes SAQCC 0366 and 30+ years, has the strongest named fault list in the set, and **does not mention the CoC on its website at all** while its Facebook page claims it issues them; its address carries a misspelt suburb and a Gauteng-style postcode ([capegasinstallations.co.za](https://capegasinstallations.co.za/)). **The LpGas Man** is the only competitor publishing a rate card and the only one with a two-year workmanship warranty, but publishes no registration number, gives **two different prices for the same geyser and fireplace jobs on the same page**, leaks Johannesburg and Durban references into its Cape Town page, and uses three separate H1 elements ([thegasman.co.za](https://thegasman.co.za/pages/gas-installations-cape-town)). **Core Gas Installations** has the cleanest schema and a real suburb-page network but publishes **no SAQCC number and no LPGSA membership** ([coregasinstallations.co.za](https://www.coregasinstallations.co.za/)). **Kemp Jonker Consulting** has the best engineering and project-management framing, claims 44+ years and 250+ installs and 50+ five-star Google reviews, and has **no SAQCC number, no LPGSA membership, no warranty and no servicing offer at all** ([kempjonkerconsulting.co.za](https://www.kempjonkerconsulting.co.za/)). **Gas Line Solutions** asserts the gas-plus-solar-plus-plumbing bundle most directly but shows a "gas geysers" category icon **with no service description behind it** ([gaslinesolutions.co.za](https://www.gaslinesolutions.co.za/)). **Top Gas and Plumbing** carries injected Czech gambling spam and testimonials dated 2019–2020 ([topgasandplumbing.co.za](https://www.topgasandplumbing.co.za/)).

So: **basic site hygiene is a differentiator in this market, not a baseline.** The review base is also thin and almost entirely self-reported — the largest counts evidenced are claims of 50+ (Kemp Jonker) and 25+ (The LpGas Man) on their own sites, third-party directories show Gas Viper at 4.5 from 11 reviews, most listed Cape Town gas installers show **zero**, and **Hellopeter's own "gas geysers installation and repairs" category shows 0 reviews and is marked Unclaimed** ([Hellopeter](https://www.hellopeter.com/gas-geysers-installation-and-repairs)). Every rating in the notes is second-hand: no Google Business Profile, Hellopeter page, SAQCC register or LPGSA register could be read directly. **Do not assert complaint patterns about this field** — no-shows, quote-versus-final-price, certificates not issued — because the review base was too thin to evidence them, and absence of complaints here is a data gap, not a clean record.

No Western Cape installer's site claims a supplier referral relationship, an "approved installer" badge from any LPG supplier, or an appliance-retailer partnership — while **Italfire publicly states that a certified gas installer must do the gas line** on its fireplace installations, which is an open, stated subcontract need ([italfire.co.za](https://italfire.co.za/fireplace-installations/)). And the estate-agency and conveyancing referral route for sale-triggered CoCs is being written about by attorneys and estate agencies while **no installer is positioned at that handoff**.

## Search demand is unmeasured; the content gaps are documented

Be clear with whoever briefs the copy: **no search volume figures were obtainable for any term in this market.** Google Keyword Planner needs an authenticated Ads account and no published South African volume dataset surfaced, so every cluster priority in the notes is reasoned from supply-side signals — the headings, URL slugs and page titles Cape Town installers and SA cost-guide publishers have actually built — and not from data. Nor could real rankings, Local Pack composition or AI Overview presence be verified, because the available search index is US-biased rather than geo-located to Cape Town. **Everything about "what ranks" in the notes is competitive-set analysis, and Sunlogic must pull Keyword Planner (geo: Cape Town / Western Cape), Search Console query data, and dated `google.co.za` SERP screenshots from a Cape Town IP before committing budget.**

What the supply-side signals do show is three demand centres — **compliance and CoC, appliance-specific installation, and cost** — with suburb geography as a thin but real secondary layer, proven monetisable by Snupit and Kandua running service-by-suburb URL templates at scale. The reasoned build order is: the core service-plus-metro money page first, then a dedicated CoC page, then one page per appliance (geyser, hob/stove, braai, fireplace), then a cost pillar, then servicing and emergency repairs, then suburb pages **only where real service evidence exists**, then comparison guides, then a separate commercial page, then safety and regulation guides. **Afrikaans should not be built.** A targeted search for `"gasinstallasie" OR "gas installateur" Kaapstad` returned **no Afrikaans-language gas installer pages at all**, which in a heavily Afrikaans province is more likely a genuine demand signal — Afrikaans speakers search in English for trade services — than a universal oversight. At most, use Afrikaans naturally inside Paarl, Stellenbosch and Durbanville page copy and revisit only if Search Console shows Afrikaans impressions.

The on-page bar is low. Of eight installer sites inspected, **two had zero structured data, one had no H1 at all, and only one (Core Gas) had valid coherent LocalBusiness-type markup**. The observed competitive band for a commercial service page is roughly **1,300–2,500 words** and for an informational cost pillar roughly **3,000+**. There is, however, a timing trap worth catching before anyone copies the leaders: both Cape Town competitors with the strongest markup implement `FAQPage`, and **Google removed FAQ rich results from Search on 7 May 2026 and deleted the documentation on 15 June 2026** ([Google Search Central changelog](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/faqpage)). Keep FAQ markup for machine comprehension, expect no visual gain, and treat anyone promising FAQ rich snippets in 2026 as selling a dead feature. `HowTo` has produced no visible result since 2023 and is cosmetically pointless. Worse, the market's best-marked-up page declares **`GasInstallationService`, a type that returns HTTP 404 on schema.org and does not exist** — there is **no gas-specific LocalBusiness subtype in schema.org 30.1 (2026-09-16)**, whose "more specific types" under `HomeAndConstructionBusiness` are exactly Electrician, GeneralContractor, HVACBusiness, HousePainter, Locksmith, MovingCompany, Plumber and RoofingContractor ([schema.org](https://schema.org/HomeAndConstructionBusiness)). Use `HomeAndConstructionBusiness` or `ProfessionalService` in one consolidated `@graph`, with `Service` nodes per offering, `areaServed` as explicit `City` nodes mirroring the Google Business Profile service areas, and **`Offer` plus `PriceSpecification` on real published prices** — the one technique the strongest page in the niche (Kandua's cost guide) uses and that **no Cape Town installer does**. `AggregateRating` and `Review` markup is implemented by **none of the seven competitors**, which is clean differentiation, but only on genuinely collected reviews.

The richest content gap is not a missing topic but a **missing arbiter**. The most-asked practical question in the market — how long a gas CoC stays valid — has at least four mutually incompatible answers circulating (no set period; two years; five years; valid until ownership changes or the installation is altered), and **three competing answers to who may issue it** (SAQCC Gas; LPGSA; and SAGA for certain combustion systems, per [SAGA Directive No. 2-2024](https://www.sagas.co.za/wp/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/SAGA-Directive-No-2-2024.pdf)). No page found resolves any of it against the actual regulation. Nobody has even explained that "Certificate of **Compliance**" and "Certificate of **Conformity**" are the same document, although both phrasings are used as search queries. Two further structural gaps are documented by real user questions with no competitor page answering them: **"convert electric geyser to gas"** and **"can I run a gas geyser alongside my existing electric geyser"** ([MyBroadband thread](https://mybroadband.co.za/forum/threads/gas-geyser-capacity-using-along-with-a-standard-geyser.1248508/)). And a third is a plain factual surprise stated once and buried: **the gas certificate covers the installation, not the appliance** — no stove, heater, braai, hob or geyser is certified by it.

For generative search, the evidence is indirect — **no AI Overview, ChatGPT or Perplexity citation could be observed for any target query**, so the checklist in the notes is inferred from which passages propagated into synthesised search answers. What propagated was consistently: short declarative sentences that stand alone when lifted; the registering body named in full on first use; the regulation cited with its number, parent act and effective date ("Regulation 17(3) of the Pressure Equipment Regulations under the Occupational Health and Safety Act 85 of 1993, effective 1 October 2009" demonstrably travels); explicit numeric ranges with the date they were quoted; and named, dated credentials — including the specific registration numbers 0366, 2786 and 5389, and one competitor's page cited precisely because it names the individual practitioner and dates his registration. Year-stamped content is visibly favoured, and a competitor still running a `south-african-gas-regulations-in-2025` slug reads as stale. Most usefully: **the synthesised answers surfaced the CoC-validity contradiction rather than hiding it, listing the competing numbers instead of picking one** — which means a page that *resolves* a contradiction with a primary citation is structurally more valuable than one that merely asserts an answer. That is the highest-leverage content play available in this niche, and it is also the one that cannot be written until the verification list below is cleared.

## Conclusion

The decisive insight is that **gas is not an energy-savings product in Cape Town — it is a compliance-and-performance product with a narrow energy case attached.** Every number in the economics points the same way: gas beats a City of Cape Town electric element geyser and nothing else, loses to a heat pump by roughly half, loses to Eskom's low-consumption tariffs outright, and loses at the hob. Meanwhile the compliance story is robust, recurring, statutory and almost entirely unsold — a certificate triggered on every change of ownership, a five-year re-inspection convention nobody is reminding anyone about, a three-layer municipal stack for restaurants that nobody explains, and a field of competitors where five of seven cannot produce a registration number. A website that leads with savings will be arguing with its own customers' arithmetic within a year. A website that leads with *"we are registered, here is the number, here is what the inspection covers, here is when yours is due again, here is the price, and here is the honest comparison including the cases where gas is the wrong answer"* has no equal in the current Western Cape field and is defensible for as long as the regulations stand.

The specific advantage Sunlogic holds is not that it can also do gas. It is that **the certificate trail and the honest comparison are both things a gas-only installer structurally cannot offer.** Only a dual-trade contractor can do the clause 9.1 electrical work inside a hob job properly rather than subcontracting it, issue the electrical and gas certificates a property transfer needs from one visit, and tell a customer that a heat pump would beat the gas geyser it is quoting on — which is precisely the kind of statement that makes the rest of the page believable. The constraint is equally specific and entirely within Sunlogic's control: **buy SANS 10087-1 Edition 7, re-read the City of Cape Town gas and tariff documents from the City's own site, get the SAQCC Gas category list and the CoC validity position in writing, and find a published South African calorific value.** Four purchases and four phone calls separate this research pack from publishable copy, and until they are done the figures in bucket (c) stay off the website — including, and especially, the ones every competitor in Cape Town is already publishing without having checked them.
